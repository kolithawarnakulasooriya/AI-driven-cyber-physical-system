Sensors for AI-Driven Cyber-Physical Systems

Kolitha Warnakulasooriya — AI Driven Cyber Physical Systems (EE 490/506)

What is Sensing?

Sensing is the process of transforming a physical quantity into a computer-readable representation that can be used for estimation, control, and higher-level decision making.

Exteroception

Perception of the external world: sensors that measure the state of the environment around the robot or CPS (e.g., range, vision, temperature).

Proprioception

Perception of the robot's own state: sensors that measure internal variables such as joint angles, velocity, forces, and battery state.

Together, exteroception and proprioception allow robots and CPS to build a consistent view of themselves and their surroundings.

Sensors in Robotics and CPS

  • Sensors provide internal feedback for closed-loop control and external interaction with the environment.
  • Animals and humans rely on distinct sensors; robotic systems emulate many of these sensing modalities.
  • A huge array of sensors is available to measure mechanical, electrical, chemical, and environmental phenomena.
  • In this course we focus on sensors used in robotics, automation, and cyber-physical systems rather than general instrumentation.

Perception Process

From raw signals to understanding, perception in robots and CPS typically follows a four-stage pipeline.

Signal Acquisition

Monitor internal or external state using appropriate sensors and sampling hardware.

Pre-processing

Filtering noise, normalizing signals, and selecting regions of interest (ROI) for further analysis.

Feature Extraction

Compute geometric and semantic features such as point clouds, edges, keypoints, or descriptors.

Interpretation

Recognition, localization, and mapping that turn features into actionable understanding.

Types of Sensors

Robotic and CPS applications use a broad taxonomy of sensors grouped by the physical quantity they measure.

  • Position: encoders, LVDT, GPS, Hall-effect sensors.
  • Velocity: accelerometers, gyroscopes, Doppler velocimeters, tachometers, encoders.
  • Force/Torque: torque sensors, strain gauges, piezoelectric sensors, load cells.
  • Tactile: tactile array sensors, piezoresistive rubber or skins.
  • Touch: limit switches, capacitive and resistive touch sensors.
  • Proximity: inductive, capacitive, photoelectric sensors.
  • Range finders: LiDAR, ultrasonic sensors, radar.
  • Vision: CCD/CMOS cameras, IR cameras, RGB-D depth cameras.
  • Sniff: gas sensors, electronic noses (E-nose).
  • Taste: pH sensors, ion-selective electrodes.
  • Speech synthesis: neural text-to-speech (TTS) systems.
  • Speech recognition: microphone arrays for voice input and beamforming.

Key Sensor Characteristics

When selecting sensors for CPS, we must consider both physical and signal-level characteristics.

  • Cost, size, and weight constraints for the platform.
  • Type of output (digital vs analog) and required interfacing (ADC/DAC, communication bus).
  • Resolution: smallest detectable change in the measured quantity.
  • Sensitivity: ratio of output change to input change; determines signal-to-noise behavior.
  • Linearity: how closely output follows a straight-line relationship with input.
  • Range: smallest and largest measurable outputs without saturation.
  • Response time: time required to reach a certain percentage of the final value.
  • Time constant: special response time corresponding to 63.2% of the total change.
  • Rise time: time from 10% to 90% of the final value.
  • Settling time: time from initial state to within 2% of the final value.
  • Frequency response: bandwidth over which the sensor accurately tracks variations.
  • Reliability, accuracy, and repeatability under operating conditions.

Position Sensors

Position sensors measure linear or angular displacement of mechanical components such as joints, shafts, and end-effectors.

  • Potentiometers: analog rotary or linear resistors used for internal feedback of joint angles.
  • Encoders: digital rotary or linear devices that count discrete steps of motion.
  • LVDT and resolvers: transformer-based sensors providing continuous analog position signals.
  • Hall-effect sensors: magnetic field sensors used for rotor position in brushless motors.
  • GPS: absolute global position for outdoor CPS platforms.

Potentiometers

Potentiometers are simple, inexpensive position sensors widely used for internal feedback in control systems.

  • Provide an analog voltage proportional to shaft or slider position.
  • Available in rotary or linear form, with single or multi-turn variants.
  • Wire-wound potentiometers produce stepwise outputs, while conductive polymer paste gives continuous output.

Encoders

Encoders convert mechanical motion into digital pulses that can be counted to estimate position, speed, and direction.

  • A disk or strip is divided into sections that alternate between opaque/transparent or reflective/non-reflective regions.
  • An LED or other light source on one side and a phototransistor on the other detect transitions as the disk moves.
  • Can be linear or rotary, with resolutions ranging from tens to many thousands of counts per revolution.

Absolute vs Incremental Encoders

Absolute Encoders

  • Each position corresponds to a unique pattern of clear and opaque sectors.
  • Provide absolute position without needing a reference homing step.
  • Higher resolutions require more output lines; often use binary or Gray code, where only one bit changes at a time.

Incremental Encoders

  • Use repeating equal sections that represent identical angle steps.
  • Measure changes in position and speed but not absolute angle without homing.
  • Direction requires two channels in quadrature (half-step out of phase) to infer rotation direction.
  • Simple to use, with few output lines and potentially very high resolution.

LVDTs and Resolvers

Linear Variable Differential Transformer (LVDT)

  • A transformer whose movable core tracks linear displacement.
  • Produces a variable analog voltage proportional to position.
  • Known for reliability, simple design, and highly linear output over its operating range.

Resolvers

  • Angular analog counterparts to LVDTs, used for measuring rotation.
  • Primary coil rotates with the shaft and carries AC excitation; two secondary coils are placed 90 degrees apart.
  • Induced voltages vary sinusoidally with angle, yielding sine/cosine outputs that can be digitized for precise angular position.

Hall-Effect Position Sensors

Hall-effect sensors measure magnetic fields and are widely used for contactless position sensing in motors and CPS.

  • The output voltage of a current-carrying conductor changes in the presence of a perpendicular magnetic field.
  • Hall transducers generate analog voltage signals that can be digitized for control applications.
  • Used extensively to sense rotor position in brushless DC motors and for proximity detection of magnets.

Velocity and Motion Sensors

Motion sensors capture linear and angular dynamics, enabling inertial navigation, stabilization, and vibration monitoring.

Accelerometers

  • Measure external forces, including gravity, along one to three axes.
  • Mechanical accelerometers use a mass-spring system; modern MEMS devices use silicon microstructures.
  • Provide low-power, multi-axis acceleration and vibration data for CPS.

Gyroscopes

  • Measure angular velocity around one or more axes to track orientation.
  • MEMS gyros rely on Coriolis forces acting on a vibrating mass, producing capacitance or piezoelectric changes.
  • Provide 1–3 axis rotational rate outputs used in IMUs and attitude estimation.

Tachometers and Doppler Velocimeters

Tachometers

  • Measure rotational speed in revolutions per minute (RPM).
  • Count rotations or pulses from a rotating element via magnetic pickup, optical beam, or generator action.
  • Can be mechanical generator-type, electronic, or non-contact optical/laser devices.

Doppler Velocimeters

  • Non-contact sensors measuring line-of-sight velocity using laser, radar, or acoustic waves.
  • Relative motion between sensor and target shifts the frequency of the reflected signal (Doppler effect).
  • Electronics demodulate the frequency shift to output real-time velocity measurements.

Force and Torque Sensing

Force and torque sensors allow robots and CPS to measure interaction forces for compliance, manipulation, and structural monitoring.

Piezoelectric Sensors

  • Piezoelectric materials generate electric charge when mechanically stressed and deform when an electric field is applied.
  • Mechanical pressure induces charge that appears as a measurable voltage across electrodes.
  • Used in vibration, impact, and dynamic force sensing with analog outputs.

Strain Gauges and Torque Sensors

  • Strain gauges convert tiny changes in length or deformation into resistance changes; often wired in Wheatstone bridges.
  • Torque sensors use pairs of force sensors placed strategically to measure torque around specific axes.
  • Multiple sensor pairs in different planes can resolve full 6-axis force/torque vectors.

Tactile and Touch Sensors

Tactile Array Sensors

  • Arrays of touch elements that report spatial patterns of contact.
  • Provide information about shape, size, and material properties of grasped objects.
  • Implementations include piezoresistive rubber skins and other pressure-sensitive matrices.

Touch and Limit Switches

  • Limit/micro switches change electrical contacts when a moving part reaches a preset mechanical position.
  • Touch sensors respond to direct physical contact, pressure, or proximity.
  • Resistive touch sensors respond to pressure between conductive layers; capacitive touch sensors detect changes in an electric field caused by a finger or conductive object.

Proximity and Ultrasonic Sensors

Proximity Sensors

  • Detect nearby objects before physical contact occurs.
  • Magnetic sensors trigger near magnets; optical sensors only see signals when within a specified range.
  • Inductive sensors detect metal, while capacitive sensors respond to objects with dielectric constants above about 1.2.

Ultrasonic Range Finders

  • Emit bursts of high-frequency sound (typically around 40 kHz) and measure echo time-of-flight to estimate distance.
  • Can operate in opposed-mode (transmitter/receiver facing) or echo-mode (single module measuring reflections).
  • Accuracy depends on sound speed, which varies with temperature and medium density, and is affected by surface reflectivity and background noise.

LiDAR Range Sensing

LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) is an active remote sensing technology that creates precise 3D maps and point clouds.

  • Emits rapid laser pulses and measures their return time to compute distance based on the speed of light.
  • Generates dense 3D point clouds for environment mapping, obstacle detection, and SLAM.
  • Common types include airborne LiDAR (ALS), terrestrial LiDAR (TLS), and bathymetric LiDAR for water surfaces.

Image, Thermal, and Hyperspectral Sensors

Image Sensors

  • Convert incident light into electrical signals to form digital images.
  • CMOS sensors are low-power and integrate processing; CCD sensors offer low noise and uniform response.
  • SPAD sensors detect single photons for ultra-low-light and time-of-flight applications.

Thermal and Hyperspectral Cameras

  • Thermal cameras detect infrared radiation using microbolometer arrays, producing heat maps.
  • Hyperspectral cameras capture hundreds of narrow spectral bands to characterize materials and conditions.
  • Applications include early crop disease detection, counterfeit drug identification, and advanced depth/3D sensing.

IMUs and Sensor Fusion

Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs) combine multiple motion sensors to provide robust estimates of orientation and motion.

  • Accelerometer: measures linear acceleration, including gravity, to infer tilt or translational motion.
  • Gyroscope: measures angular velocity (rotation rate) around axes.
  • Magnetometer: detects magnetic fields to provide heading relative to Earth's magnetic north.
  • Sensor fusion algorithms such as Kalman filtering combine these signals to reduce noise and drift and produce stable pose estimates.

Microphones and Audio Sensing

Microphones convert acoustic energy (sound waves) into electrical energy for analysis and interaction.

  • Used for acoustic source localization, voice interfaces, and environmental sound monitoring.
  • Beamforming with microphone arrays enables directional hearing and noise suppression by weighting signals across the array.
  • Audio sensing is critical for human-robot interaction, speech recognition, and surveillance applications.

From Physical World to AI Decisions

Sensors transform physical phenomena into data streams that feed estimation, control, and learning algorithms in modern cyber-physical systems.

Understanding sensor principles, characteristics, and limitations is essential for designing robust, safe, and intelligent CPS.
